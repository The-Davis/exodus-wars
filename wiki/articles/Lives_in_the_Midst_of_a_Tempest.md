Some time in the early Tempest War

by Seth Dickinson

=====Alamos Heights, Albanus=====
     The galaxy has changed a lot in the past few years. The [[Second Exodus War]] killed billions and left the political landscape of human space changed forever. The war’s devastation was unmatched, but there were also many positive changes: new technologies, new worlds, and new divisions of humanity emerged during the struggle.
     Given the dramatic events still occurring across known space, it’s easy to overlook ordinary people. The Galactic Herald’s own Rebeka Avishag traveled to major worlds of each government in known space, documenting the lives of common citizens and painting a vivid picture of their day-to-day lives. 
     The first part of this series takes us to Albanus, the thriving hub of the Prefecture’s Ueda District, where the Griffith family of Alamos Heights agreed to an interview with Ms. Avishag.

     Rebeka Avishag’s Reporting
     Alamos Heights is a city that has sprung full-grown out of nothing. The vast influx of immigrants from devastated Terra in the past few years created a massive demand for housing and work, one that Rahn Industries was happy to fill. The twin cities of Ishkandar and Alamos Heights were planned and funded entirely by the star-spanning super-corporation, Ishkandar as a research center, Alamos Heights as a manufacturing hub. 
     The Griffith home is located in the middle-class suburbs to the north of the city center. It’s built in the Interstellar style, a simple architectural school popularized by the post-war colonization boom. The Griffiths are in the middle of painting their home a pleasant cream color, but they haven’t made much progress; the house is still mostly covered by the cheap spray-on sealant that is used to weatherproof low-budget housing.
     Kyle and Ilona Griffith greet me as I step out of my car. Kyle is in his early 40s, muscular and fit, his face earnest and rough-edged; Ilona, his wife of ten years, is dark-haired and dark-eyed, Russian-ethnic.
As we walk to the front door, I find that Kyle and Ilona are not shy people: they ask me to begin the questions right away. I inquire about their history.
     “I was born on Earth, in Britain,” Kyle explains. “When I was a child, I loved politics and debate, so it was a natural step for me to become a lawyer. I worked closely with a lot of United Earth Alliance officials, and eventually I became a government employee. That was when I met Ilona.”
     “I was a government functionary,” Ilona explains. “I did data-management and statistics, and I ended up assigned to Kyle’s office. We hated each other at first.” She laughs at the memory. “Nobody could believe it when we announced our engagement.” 
     They both remember exactly where they were when Lucius Black’s Exiles returned to Terra. “It was like something had…come out of the history books and grabbed us,” Ilona recalls, her eyes distant. “Nobody could believe it. There wasn’t any panic, at first; nobody knew what it meant. Then, almost before we knew it, there were ships coming out of the sky; I remember watching the contrails overhead.”
     Kyle stops by the door, as if this kind of discussion doesn’t belong inside the house. “When I heard that the President had been killed trying to escape, I realized that we were being invaded, as ridiculous as it sounded, and that whoever these people were – we weren’t entirely certain they were the Torimur colonists at the time – they didn’t like the [[United Earth Alliance]]. Ilona and I ran; she had friends in Moscow, near our office, and we hid with them. It was a good thing, too; everyone else in our office was rounded up by Omegas and killed.”
     “We had to hide our identities very carefully for a long time.” The memories are clearly still fresh and painful; there is a catch in Ilona’s voice as she speaks. “If anyone learned that we had been members of the old government, we would have been arrested and probably killed. We both picked up other trades during the occupation; Kyle worked in a manufacturing plant, and I became a PLC technician, working on the programmable logic controllers in those same plants.”
     “The last few months of the Civil War were pure hell. Ilona realized, early on in the war, that Moscow would be a strategic target, and it’s a good thing she did – we were one of the few families actually prepared when the fighting started. We dug a bunker and stocked it with food; some of my co-workers helped us make sure it was secure. We owe those people our lives."
     Moscow was the focus of intense ground fighting as well as nuclear attacks.
     "Kyle and I spent three continuous months in that bunker. Early on in the war, we could occasionally leave during lulls in the fighting, maybe scavenge some supplies – but later on the combat became so heavy that we almost died of thirst. Those bunker days were miserable; we were starved, filthy, and emaciated. The temperature at night was so low that we froze to the ground a few times. Finally, a group of Prefecture soldiers stumbled onto our bunker after they got separated from their unit.”
     “I’d love to say we picked up arms and fought with them,” Kyle says, laughing ruefully, “but we were all so battered and terrified that all we could think of was getting out of the city. The soldiers were in our bunker for almost a week; every morning they’d head out to try to link up with their unit, and every evening they’d return with fewer men. Finally, they found a working radio and called for airborne extraction. The Skyhawk that came for them had a few empty seats, and they let us ride along – why, I don’t know; maybe they were grateful to us, maybe they just thought we could give them intelligence. Whatever the reason, they saved us. Two days after we left, a nuclear strike annihilated the city.”
     Of the twenty million people in Moscow at the beginning of the Imperial Civil War, two million survived; a staggering ninety percent of the city’s inhabitants died as a result of the ground fighting, the nuclear attacks, or the radioactive fallout that resulted. The Griffiths realize how lucky they were.
     “Terra was pretty much ruined after the war, and there was nothing left for us there. When we heard there were job openings, we decided to emigrate to New Albany.” Kyle gestures expansively. “So here we are.” 
     The Griffith’s history, dramatic as it might seem, shares many common themes with the life story of most Terrans. The Second Exodus War touched every pocket of the Terran race, from the most remote colony to Terra itself, and no one escaped unscathed. The Griffiths have adjusted well, but for many, constant survivor’s guilt is a day-to-day reality.
     “Yes, we remember the people who died,” Ilona says, nodding matter-of-factly. “But Kyle and I never allowed ourselves to get torn up over them. Our philosophy was to make the best we could of the situation we were in. Of course it hurt to see our friends, even our family members, disappearing or dying – but we knew they’d want us to keep on going. We didn’t let their memories drag us down; they just motivated us to try harder.”
     “I’m not proud of all the things we did during the war. A lot of people stumbled onto our bunker, and we turned all of them away, even some people we knew.” Kyle seems to be staring into infinity, the memories of his time in Moscow are so vivid that they take on a visceral immediacy. “A few people tried to force their way in, and we beat them off with our bare hands. But we tried to help all the others; we set aside some of our supplies to give to other people, we gave them all directions to other places where refugees had gathered. When the fighting wasn’t bad, we’d go out and look for wounded people in the rubble. There were other bunkers nearby, too, and we had a community; we’d pool our supplies and divide them up as needed. We never lost our humanity, and that – that I’m proud of.” 
     There is a moment of silence as each of us descends into introspection. It is impossible for me to imagine what the Griffiths are thinking, but I am profoundly humbled by their story. It would have been so easy for them to descend into savagery, but they and their fellow survivors – total strangers – worked together to keep the spark of civilization alive. Kyle and Ilona Griffith are living testaments to the tenacity and strength of the human spirit.
     “So,” Ilona asks, her voice brittle, “shall we show you the house?”
     The Griffith’s home is almost Spartan; there are no pictures up, only a bland placeholder pattern programmed into the wallpaper.
     “We haven’t had much time to decorate,” Ilona explains, gesturing at the bare walls. “After all our time in camps, it’s just nice to have heat and running water again.”
     But there are signs that the Griffiths are making themselves at home: fresh-bought furniture in the living room – “Not quite how I want it yet,” Ilona laughs. There’s also a single luxury – an Icronian projector set. “We’re news junkies,” Kyle explains. “I wasted hours on the local Internet when we first arrived here. Ilona made me promise to stop if she bought me a new projector – but now that we’ve got a big screen, we both end up browsing.”
     The two share a study next to their bedroom; their old projector, Rahn Industries corporate surplus, rests on Kyle’s desk. He yanks the data chip from the drive and shows it to me: an Icronian language teacher program. “I’m hoping to get back into law eventually,” he explains, “maybe working for RI – but they want me to learn Icronian first.” 
     “I hope he does better than he did with Russian,” Ilona grumbles, “or he’ll be a plant worker forever.”
     We sit down in the kitchen; I cannot help but notice that there are three boxes of preserved foods and several gallons of water stored in one of the cupboards. Ilona dials the kitchen for coffee, and we sit around the table. It quickly becomes obvious that the Griffiths have very developed political views. Kyle is a strong supporter of the Prefecture government. 
     “We Terrans finally have a government that gets the job done,” he opines, sipping at his coffee. “That was one of the things that I used to waste all my time watching – the public records of the goings-on in the capital on Ueda; it’s just fascinating to watch how different things are now, compared to my time in the UEA. The only problem is, the laws are so simple that they’ve rendered lawyers like me obsolete.” 
     Ilona isn’t so sure. “The United Earth Alliance was the end product of a long process of evolution,” she points out. “It was a government that was tried and tested by time. The Prefecture’s structure is such a radical departure – it seems like a great risk to take. I’m also uncomfortable leaving so many former Imperial officials in power; just because they happened to end up on the winning side of the Civil War doesn’t mean they should be excused for their crimes.” 
     “We fight about this a lot.” Kyle laughs. “It’s an odd time we’re living in. There’s so much destruction, but so much potential as well. Everyone at the factory talks about how much they’ve lost, but they’re also very pragmatic about it. We’re all looking forward to see what happens next, I think.”
     What about the Volucris? Kyle and Ilona both shrug. “After the Exodus War, I figure a bunch of bugs will be a relative pushover for the likes of Prefect Hadrian.” Even Ilona must agree: “If there’s one advantage to putting all these soldiers in positions of power, it’s that they can keep us safe.”
     But when I mention the recent, tragic destruction of Leskovik by the Mesarthrim, the mood shifts. Other human beings seem far more terrifying than an alien menace. “We’ve lived through one nuclear war,” Ilona reminds me. “We could handle another one. Rahn Industries helps keep the planet well-defended, and if the war comes here…well, we’re ready.”
     Indeed, the two of them seem ready for another confrontation. Kyle and Ilona both carry sidearms, and Ilona has a rifle in her closet. They’re also in the process of filing permits to build a bunker in their backyard.
     “We’re hopeful,” Kyle says, trying to wrap up their worldview in a single sentence. “But if things go wrong, we’ll be prepared.”
