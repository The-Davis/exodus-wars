The Field Gun's long and successful career was put in jeopardy by the formation of the Marine Corps. While the weapon could put out more firepower than any other infantry weapon, it was not mobile enough to satisfy the needs of the Corps.

Two results came into play. The first was a design submitted by the Mikotyr-Deerfield Corporation.

The problem with firing a kinetic round with Gauss at the required round per minute rate is the housing size of a Gauss propulsion system increases exponentially after 2000 meters per second and 3000 rounds per minute.

The Deerfield company was a new startup, launched by several firearms engineers who were off-world when Leskovik was bombed. They proposed a compact design that would meet the Corps' requirements: 3000 meters per second and 4000 rounds per minute.

The first prototype, the Deerfield Squad Automatic Weapon Prototype 1, was enormous. It would barely fit on a heavy Rig when armed with sufficient ammunition, let alone an un-augmented infantry trooper. This was merely a proof of concept model, not a working prototype.

The D-2 prototype was built using an Ikronin repulsor system instead of Gauss. The D-1 and D-2 models were benchmarked against each other, and it was determined that with sufficient power-efficiency tweaks to the repulsor design, the SAW model would work.

[[Image:M11SAW.jpg]]

Unfortunately, no one in the Deerfield company had sufficient experience with repulsor technology to create an efficient design. They brought in Ikronin assistance, in the form of the Mikotyr Engineering Firm.

Mikotyr was another off-shoot, engineers who broke away from the Förakrig Corporation after the firm suffered terrible losses in the war with Volucris.

The resulting merger was the Mikotyr-Deerfield Corporation. Their combined efforts brought five more prototypes before the final design, the MiD-7 SAW, was accepted by the Marine Corps.

The MiD-7 SAW can be loaded, carried and fired by one infantry trooper. It is a belt-fed design, firing from 1000-round belts that are wrapped around a machine-driven feeder. The design is extremely efficient, and weighs only 35 kilograms fully loaded. An operator can carry one additional belt without having to sacrifice the rest of his field gear.
