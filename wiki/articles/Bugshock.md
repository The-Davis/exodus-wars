By August [[2334]] there was no place in the universe more hostile to human life than the surface of [[Lorithan]].

Look, I know what you’re thinking. It’s absurd, right? Pure hyperbole. The delusional construct of a damaged mind, the fantasy of a soldier desperate to believe that his personal suffering was somehow special. We all want to believe that we were the ones in the most pain. It allows us to believe that we are individuals instead of cogs, machines, drones.

Never mind. Let me deconstruct your misperception.

What about a bomb? Standing under a bomb is, we can probably agree, pretty lethal. But ask yourself this. What will that bomb do once it has killed you? Will it spawn a clutch of new bombs from the ruin of your corpse, then go on to bomb your friends?

I thought not.

Is the interior of a nuclear fireball more hazardous than the surface of Lorithan? The fireball will sear the flesh from your bones and then unravel the molecules of your body like rotten cobwebs. But in a ship, in [[Ixoarchada]] battle armor, with the right tools – you could survive. And the fireball will pass. It will fade. You will be left on the burnt black char, alone, a monument to human ingenuity.

Fine, then. A leap into a black hole is certain death. Unavoidable. Correct?

But the singularity will not hunt you down. It will not learn. It will not teach its myriad silent scurrying spawn to fight and eat and lust for the feel of metal and bone breaking beneath the mandible. It will not -

You see where I’m going here.

Bugshock, some of us called it. Post-traumatic stress caused by exposure to a form of life so voraciously alien that it makes us wonder if discrete, sapient, conscious organisms are actually evolution’s most survivable product. Talk about existential angst!

The bugshock has me. It hit me hard.  What I saw on Lorithan convinced me that mankind is a sad and transient intermediary between two forms of mindless hunger.

By the time we evacuated Lorithan the machines owned that world. Some of them were the bugs. Some of them were ours. There was no place left on Lorithan for human life.

We had been rendered obsolete, and a new ecology had begun to thrive.

It was our fault we were cut off. We had missed the first call to retreat, our communications jammed by plasma haze in the upper atmosphere, by battle damage, and by the sheer mass of bugs. The Ixoarchada ‘covering our flank’ – by that I mean killing every bug within a hundred kilometers while we waited for target calls that would never come – pulled back in a gorgeous sweep of scarred hovercraft. We hesitated when they left, unwilling to abandon our earthworks and bunkers, thinking it was another lightning deployment on their part. We were squandering our last chance, and by the time we realized the retreat had come early, the bugs were in behind us, and beneath us.

They came out of tunnels in the middle of the bivouac. Seismic missed them – what good is seismic on a world rocked by constant bombardment? We were smart enough to keep sentries and SPAR autoturrets at the ready, and they killed a few hundred, maybe a thousand of the smaller Racer breeds. Nothing, in other words.

I was talking to Lieutenant Shapoza near the command track, and then the guns and ‘perimeter’ alarms went off. The first breakout was by the aid station – maybe they could smell the wounded?  Flares went up and I fired my rifle at harsh chemical shadows that might have been bugs. There was gunfire everywhere, and you could kind of tell it was not good gunfire, because there was too much full-auto panic.

A suicide Racer, a Curvicosa Beta, blew up under the command track and flipped it on its side. I saw Captain Liu die in full battle rattle, his armored legs pinned beneath the track, an enormous shard of anti-Slicer grating through his abdomen. He was alive still, firing his rifle in calm controlled bursts, but his armor must have dosed him so heavily that he could not feel the immature Hunter that bit through his armor, slit his belly open and began, to unspool his intestines – 

God.

Give me a moment.

To sum up, we were all going to die. The [[Volucris]] were in amongst the formation. Someone must have been calling for air or jump infantry, but nothing was coming. Here and there a noncom pulled together a fighting position, but the concentration of scent and sound only gave the Volucris a target to congregate on.
