The '''Anti-Missile System''' (AMS) can be a reflex weapon--one which requires no guidance from either the pilot or the main computer--or a slaved weapon--one which fully integrates with the computer systems.

In reflex configuration, the AMS fires as soon as it detects incoming missiles. It has about five seconds to shoot down missiles, and has a 60% success rate.

In slave configuration, the main computer tracks the incoming missile, and utilizes chaff and flares first. If these countermeasures fail, the AMS is activated. The AMS has a mere two seconds to shoot down the missile. However, the computer usually has a Barrier or Particle Shield to activate as a final line of defense. The slaved AMS has a 45% success rate.

Ammo-based AMS - The ammo-based AMS fires very small bullets to shoot down missiles. These bullets are smaller than machine gun ammo, and are similar to shot for a shotgun. Ammo-based AMS run cool, but eventually run out of ammo.

Laser-based AMS - The laser-based AMS is a very advanced, very expensive system. It uses a pair of Micro Pulse Lasers to shoot down incoming missiles. The laser-based AMS runs very hot, and must power down periodically to avoid overheating. Laser-based AMS are only used on high-ranking officer's Mechs and Aerospace fighters.

The Anti-Missile System is a reflex weapon. It has completely independent target acquisition, tracking, and firing systems. The AMS begins tracking as soon as it detects incoming missiles. It takes it 0.05 seconds to determine which missiles are most likely to hit the AMS' ship or Rig, then the AMS opens fire. An AMS has a 75% shoot-down rate, but the last couple missiles often blow up at such close proximity that the armor or shields of the ship still take some damage. On warships, multiple AMS' are linked together to split up the missile interdiction duties. A warship's AMS network has an 85% success rate on standard missiles, though most anti-warship missiles have evasive programming.
